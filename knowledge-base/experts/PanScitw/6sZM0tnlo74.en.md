Kind: captions Language: en Without EUV, will the semiconductor industry be over? This EUV machine costs 181 million USD. Not only is it expensive, it also occupies a pivotal position in the chip industry chain. Under the restrictions of the U.S. CHIPS Act, ASML is prohibited from exporting EUV machines to China. Even its EUV machines in Taiwan have kill switches installed. ASML has prepared for the worst, making sure that this technology cannot be stolen even China invades Taiwan. But why exactly is this machine sought after by the whole world? Can't we just build another company like ASML? The key technology of making chips has been around since 1957. It is essentially stacking materials on silicon wafers. Only that each layer of material needs to be etched with patterns through photolithography before the next layer stacks on. So does the next layer. To ensure patterns on chips are correct, in 1957, Lathrop developed the photolithography process. Let's do a quick review of this. First, grow the desired material on a wafer. Then coat it with photoresist. The next step—exposure—is the key. Place a photomask between the UV light source and the wafer. The mask is with a carved-out pattern. The mask itself blocks UV light. The UV light passing through the hollow part will change the chemical properties of the photoresist. Take negative photoresist for example. The photoresist exposed to UV light becomes insoluble. When rinsed with a solvent, these parts remain. The parts that were not exposed to the UV due to the mask's blocking will be rinsed away. Finally, add hydrofluoric acid or other solvents. The area on the wafer not protected by photoresist will be etched by the solvent with desired patterns. Now, congratulations, one lithography process is completed. You have printed the first layer of a chip. Next, just repeat the process again and again. Finally, you should build 3D chips on the wafer. When we want to make smaller chips, we should try to leave finer patterns on the photoresist. In the past, simply shrinking the size of the mask allows patterns to be smaller and smaller. But when a mask shrinks to a certain degree, diffraction occurs. To be precise, when the slits of a mask are thinner than half the light's wavelength, the light diffracts. When passing through such slits, the light will not only go straight, but also make a turn at the edge of the slits, casting the wrong pattern. In other words, although we can keep shrinking the mask, but to ensure the accuracy of a pattern, the size of each feature within cannot be smaller than half the wavelength of the light. For example, in the early 2000s, the light source used at that time was laser. Its wavelength had been reduced from krypton fluoride's (KrF2) 248 nanometers to argon fluoride's (ArF) 193 nanometers. This 193-nanometer laser is of course inadequate to the task for current sub-7nm processes. To get around this physical limit, scientists tried various methods such as immersion lithography and multiple patterning. Immersion lithography is to spread a layer of water or other liquid between the light source and the wafer to improve the path of light. This is a bit like, when using a high-magnification microscope, we would put a drop of immersion oil between the lens and the sample. Multiple patterning is to divide a pattern into multiple exposures. For example, if there are 6 lines—1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6,— to be exposed, the lines can be divided into two passes. The first pass exposes line 1, 3, and 5. The second 2, 4, and 6. This act of adding the spacing between lines can reduce diffraction as much as possible. These methods are collectively called resolution enhancement techniques—RET. They are meant to achieve higher resolution at a given wavelength by redesigning lithography processes. In addition, upon diffraction, the UV light deviates more and more as the distance between the mask and the wafer increases. In the 1970s, some researchers placed the mask right on the wafer, making the mask touch the photoresist to minimize the distance between the two. But with this method, when the mask was lifted after exposure, it was usually glued with photoresist and damaged the wafer. Eventually, this approach was abandoned. Researchers turned to developing the mask aligner instead. Using precision equipment, they can finely adjust the distance between the mask and the wafer and other parameters to reduce diffraction. With these improvements, although the light source at that time was the deep ultraviolet laser—DUV— with a 193-nanometer wavelength, amazingly, we still advanced all the way to the 7nm process node. This deserves a round of applause. But below 7 nanometers, DUV lithography ultimately met its bottleneck. Scientists still had to resort to light with shorter wavelengths. You might think that what's so difficult about that? It is incredibly difficult. For example, light must be focused through lenses, but ordinary glass lenses absorb short-wavelength UV light. Ultraviolet light with wavelengths below 185 nanometers can even be absorbed by air. Therefore, the entire lithography process must be conducted in a vacuum. If you dig into these issues, you'll find more difficulties. Anyway, some researchers were finally determined to develop a light with less than one-tenth the wavelength, the 13.5-nanometer extreme ultraviolet light—EUV. Thus began a great project that would take 30 years, cost billions of dollars, and turned out to be achieved by only one company in the world. In previous episodes, we mentioned that the leader of the photolithography equipment market kept changing. Since Lathrop invented this technology, From Texas Instruments, PerkinElmer, GCA, to Nikon and Canon in the 1980s... Successively, they have been the key manufacturers at different times. Eventually, in the 1990s, a crucial period, a new challenger, ASML, emerged. Nikon, Canon, and ASML became the three giants competing in the lithography equipment market. However, a decision made by Intel altered the competitive landscape. At that time, the lab led by Intel and the US government believed that Japan's semiconductor industry was developing rapidly and that many businesses once belonging to the U.S. were snatched away by Japan. If they wanted to invest in an equipment manufacturer, they should choose the Dutch one, which was more neutral. Therefore, Intel decided to partner with ASML, starting a 30-year-long R&amp;D collaboration. Although this project required a huge amount of time, money, and energy, the massive investment would put ASML in an unshakable position as a leader in photolithography 30 years later. Intel hoped ASML could develop an EUV equipment featuring 13.5 nanometer light to address smaller process nodes in the future. But just figuring out how to generate a steady 13.5 nm light source was an enormous challenge because not a laser source or light bulb can directly emit 13.5-nanometer light. To emit EUV, there must be high-energy plasma. The way to produce it is using high-intensity lasers to hit the target, making the target instantly generate the plasma that emits EUV. For the mission of producing EUV with plasma, ASML chose another laser-making company, Cymer, to do the job. Cymer's task was not simple at all. They had to design a process that uses carbon dioxide lasers to bombard tin metal to generate EUV. All in all, this process is certainly much more difficult than just lighting a bulb. First, the tin metal must be turned into Sn droplet of 30 micrometers in diameter. and be shot out by an emitter at a speed of 300 kilometers per hour through a vacuum chamber. There's more. Each Sn droplet will be hit by a high-power carbon dioxide laser twice. The first hit is for heating the Sn droplet and making it expand. The second one heats it up to 220,000 degrees Celsius, turning it into a scorching plasma whose temperature is fourty times then that of the sun’s surface. As such, this high-temperature plasma will radiate short-wavelength ultraviolet light, which is EUV. However, this is just a short EUV pulse. To generate a steady streak of EUV, this process needs to be repeated 50,000 times per second. In building this bombardment equipment, what was just described was just about 1% of the difficulty. For example, just to create this laser, Cymer went to another laser-making company —TRUMPF. TRUMPF took 10 years to build this laser. Eventually, the laser emitter consists of more than 450,000 parts. Why make it so complicated? It turns out that after the laser hits the Sn droplet the tin will be blasted into debris which affects EUV generation. Therefore, the Sn droplet must move fast and the laser must be precisely controlled for perfect timing so that the Sn droplet can keep entering the reaction chamber to generate steady EUV light. As an aside, in addition to tin, they have also considered other elements— for example, xenon plasma that can emit a shorter EUV wavelength of only 11 nanometers. But the EUV intensity emitted by xenon was insufficient. Tin was still the best choice. Up to this point, only half the problems are solved. The other half lies in the lithography process. The light still needs to pass through a series of lens sets to be cast on chips. The biggest challenge here is that EUV is easily absorbed by substances. To avoid the EUV reacting with air, the chamber is pumped into a vacuum. This is probably the simplest step of all. The real challenge is that even lenses themselves absorb EUV. This top-level challenge should be dealt with by a top-notch company. The leading company in optics from Germany, Zeiss, undertook this task. First, they had to rule out using lens' refraction as a way to focus the light because as long as EUV passes through lenses, it will be greatly attenuated by absorption. Therefore, optical columns in EUV machines are an all-reflective system which is entirely composed of mirrors. Although being reflective, mirrors can still absorb EUV. Eventually, Zeiss coated the mirrors with two types of layers —silicon and molybdenum— overlapping each other dozens to a hundred times, forming a device called the Bragg mirror. When light goes through these layers with alternating refractive indices, the Bragg mirror can raise its reflectivity by creating more reflection and constructive interference. Now this may sound feasible, but it is still a very very very difficult task. This episode is sponsored by Micro-Epsilon Taiwan. Thanks to them, we are able to realize the pinnacle of human technology. To accurately cast the EVU at the target, these mirrors must be incredibly smooth. How smooth? If the mirrors were scaled up to let’s say the size of the entire Taiwan island, the height deviation on the mirrors can only be less than 0.01 millimeter. That's about the width of a hair. The optical precision of this system is like standing on Earth and trying to aim a laser pointer at a ping pong ball on the Moon. Only through this, the hard-earned EUV light can be reflected and focused multiple times to pass right through the nanometer-scale photomask and land on the photoresist. Intel's Director of Lithography, Mark Phillips, once said EUV is the most precise instrument ever made by humans. It is the ultimate manifestation of human technology. For real-time control and correction of these mirror sets, masks, and the movement of the wafer stages ultra-high precision sensors are required. Devices such as capacitive sensors, eddy current inductive sensors, or optical ones such as confocal chromatic sensors and white light interferometer —sensors of different measurement mechanisms— can cater to different circumstances and materials, achieving sub-nanometer precision. They are the core measurement tools in many precision industries such as the semiconductor industry. The manufacture of many sensors in EUV machines is entrusted to Micro-Epsilon for customized development. In addition to the well-known chromatic confocal technology, there are other optical and non-optical methods to monitor such a high-tech beast like the EUV machine. For example, if a mirror set moves slightly, the capacitive sensors will sense the displacement through changes in the dielectric. In fact, there are nearly a thousand sensors from Micro-Epsilon in an EUV machine. Inside it, not only the mirror set need correction but also the photomask. If its position is slightly off, defects will occur when the pattern is being down-sized and printed on wafers. This will subsequently affect yield. After all, current semiconductors are so small that even a few layers of atoms will make a big difference. In addition, the position of the wafer in relation to the mask also matters. Since we can't move the laser and optical parts that have been painstakingly aligned, the wafer becomes the one part that can be moved during exposure. The dual-stage system of the EUV machine can move two wafers simultaneously. One undergoes exposure while the other is measured for precision at the same time. The stages move at high speed with six degrees of freedom. The sensors used here are eddy current sensors with nanometer-level precision.
